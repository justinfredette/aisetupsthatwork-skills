# Say no to scope creep without losing the client

## When to use

Freelancers and solo service businesses whose clients keep adding extras to a fixed-price project.

## Steps

1. Write your scope in outcomes and counts: deliverables, revisions, meetings, formats. Add an exclusions list.
2. When a new request arrives, do not answer on the spot. Say you will check it against the scope and reply in writing.
3. Paste the request and your scope into the prompt below to get a polite pushback script and a change-order draft.
4. Send the client two options: do the extra work for a stated price, or swap it for something already in scope.
5. End every call with a three-bullet recap email (decisions, owners, dates) so verbal add-ons do not quietly become scope.
6. Save your best scripts. Reused pushback takes minutes instead of dread.

## Prompt

Fill the [BRACKETED] parts with the user, then follow this prompt:

```
I am a freelancer. A client just asked for [DESCRIBE THE NEW REQUEST]. My agreed scope is: [PASTE SCOPE]. The new request is not in it. Draft two things: 1) a short, warm reply that names the request as new work, points back to the agreed scope, and offers to do it as a change order with a price placeholder; 2) a one-paragraph change-order template with fields for description, price, and timeline impact. Keep the tone friendly and firm. Show me both drafts before I send anything.
```

## Guardrails

- Draft first and show the user. Never send, post, pay, book, cancel or delete without the user's explicit approval of that exact action.
- Use only facts the user gives you or that you can cite. If something is missing, ask instead of guessing.

## Common snags

- AI cannot know your contract terms. Paste the actual scope so the script references the right details.
- Generic scripts sound cold. Fill in the bracketed fields with the real request and client name.
- Pushback over the phone with no written follow-up leaves you with no record.
