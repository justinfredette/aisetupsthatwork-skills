# Prep a D&D session with AI

## When to use

Dungeon Masters who want a solid session in under an hour instead of a scattered evening of prep.

## Steps

1. Ask your players what they plan to do next session. Prep guides agree this matters most: it narrows everything you prepare to the area they will actually visit.
2. Write a two-sentence recap of last session: where the party is, what just happened, which threads are open.
3. Feed the recap plus the party’s plans into the prompt. Ask for the six prep categories DMs use: Locations, Characters, Scenes, Secrets, Monsters, and Loot, each with brief 1 to 3 sentence descriptions.
4. Get your secrets list. The Lazy DM checklist calls for about ten secrets and clues per session: single-sentence facts, kept separate from how the party might discover them.
5. Ask for one strong start: a single descriptive sentence framing where the session begins. Then outline potential scenes in a few words per line, without getting attached to them.
6. Plan one or two fantastic locations per hour of play, each with an evocative name and three descriptive aspects. For NPCs, keep it to a name, their connection to the story, and an archetype.
7. Read the whole thing once and cut half. Anything the party will never reach is clutter. Keep the prep to one page you can glance at mid-session.

## Prompt

Fill the [BRACKETED] parts with the user, then follow this prompt:

```
Help me prep my next D&D session. I am the DM.

Last session recap: [e.g. "The party cleared the goblin warren under Thornford and learned the stolen grain shipments were bound for Castle Dusk"]
Where the party is headed: [e.g. "They plan to travel to Castle Dusk to investigate"]
Party: [e.g. "4 players, level 5: fighter, cleric, rogue, wizard"]
Session length: [e.g. "3 hours"]

Give me session prep organized as:
1. Strong start: one descriptive sentence framing where the session begins.
2. Locations: 1-2 fantastic locations per hour of play. Each: evocative name plus three descriptive aspects.
3. Characters: NPCs they will likely meet. Each: name, connection to the story, one-line archetype.
4. Scenes: potential scenes, a few words per line. Do not over-plan; the players will surprise me.
5. Secrets and clues: about 10 single-sentence facts the party could discover.
6. Monsters and encounters: encounters that fit the scenes and location. Treat challenge ratings as a loose gauge.
7. Loot: what they might find, nothing campaign-breaking.

Rules:
- Keep every entry to 1-3 sentences. This must fit on one page.
- Do not decide what the players do. Prep situations, not plots.
- Flag anything that contradicts my recap.

Review the prep before the session. I run the game; this is raw material, not a script.
```

## Guardrails

- Draft first and show the user. Never send, post, pay, book, cancel or delete without the user's explicit approval of that exact action.
- Use only facts the user gives you or that you can cite. If something is missing, ask instead of guessing.

## Common snags

- The AI writes a novel instead of prep. Enforce the one-page rule: if you cannot glance at it mid-session, it is too long.
- It invents lore that contradicts your campaign. Feed it your recap and story bible, and ask it to flag contradictions.
- Over-prepped encounters that never happen. That is normal; file the unused material for a later session instead of forcing it in.
